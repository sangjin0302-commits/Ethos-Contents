Information verified as of October 15, 2026.

> Three-point summary
>
> 1. A change in an E-7 employee's salary, duties or worksite does not always require a new visa, but revising the employment contract does not replace any required immigration procedure.
>
> 2. Salary must be assessed against applicable wage requirements; actual duties against the authorized occupation; and a worksite move against the workplace permission and reporting rules.
>
> 3. For a planned change, check whether permission must come first. If the change has already taken effect, establish the actual dates and determine which obligations applied at that time.

A Korean company wants to reduce a foreign employee's salary, transfer that employee to another department, and move the employee from head office to a regional branch. The employee already holds E-7 status, the employer remains the same legal entity, and the current period of stay has not expired.

Can the company implement everything by signing an amended employment contract and issuing a transfer notice?

**No. A contract amendment does not, by itself, satisfy immigration requirements.** But the opposite claim—that every internal change requires a fresh visa or a report within 15 days—is also incorrect.

The appropriate procedure depends on what actually changes.

Consider a hypothetical trading company based in Seoul. An E-7-1 employee has been managing overseas clients and export-related business at its Seoul headquarters. After a reorganization, the employer proposes the following:

1. Reduce the employee's annual salary from KRW 36 million to KRW 30 million.
2. Move the employee from overseas sales into domestic sales, changing the main duties.
3. Transfer the employee from the Seoul headquarters to a branch in Gyeonggi Province.

These decisions belong to the same HR plan, but they raise different legal questions: an occupation-specific wage threshold, the activities permitted under the approved E-7 occupation, and the status of the new worksite.

The employer should assess those questions separately rather than treat them all as a single "contract change."

## 1. First establish whether the change is planned or already effective

### Before the changes take effect

When the employee is still performing the original duties at the approved workplace, HR can review the proposed terms before setting an implementation date.

Start by recording four facts: the new salary and payment terms, the work the employee will actually perform, the location where that work will take place, and the intended effective date. Compare each of them with the employee's existing E-7 approval and employment documents.

If the proposed salary falls below the wage standard applicable to the occupation, examine the immigration implications before making the reduction. If the actual duties change, compare them with the activities covered by the approved occupation. If the move constitutes a workplace change requiring prior permission, do not start work at the new site before that permission is granted.

The practical sequence is **compare the existing approval and proposed terms, identify the required procedure, and then confirm the implementation date**.

### If the company has already implemented the changes

The starting point is different when the employee has already joined the new department or started work at the new branch.

Determine when the salary actually changed, when the new duties began, and when the employee first worked at the new location. Those facts matter more than an intended date recorded in a later document.

Then examine the permission or reporting duties that applied at the relevant time, including the applicable deadline and any statutory exclusion. Rewriting the date on an amended contract does not retroactively complete a procedure that should already have been performed.

Equally, a change is not automatically a violation merely because no report was made. The first question is whether that particular change triggered a legal duty at all.

### If only the department name changed

Suppose "Overseas Sales" was renamed "International Business" while the employee's duties, work location and contract period remained unchanged.

A change of department name alone does not necessarily require a new immigration status. The relevant inquiry is whether the authorized activities or legally reportable particulars have changed in substance.

## 2. Does a salary reduction affect E-7 wage eligibility?

On December 29, 2025, the Ministry of Justice issued Announcement No. 2025-406 concerning the 2026 wage requirements for the E-7 status.

The published general annual thresholds are:

- E-7-1, professional workers: at least KRW 31.12 million.
- E-7-2, semi-professional workers: at least KRW 25.89 million.
- E-7-3, general skilled workers: at least KRW 25.89 million.
- E-7-4, skilled workers: at least KRW 26 million.

**The announced period of application is February 1 through December 31, 2026.** A different wage requirement applies where the Minister of Justice has established an occupation-specific standard, including on grounds such as protecting domestic employment.

Return to the hypothetical E-7-1 employee. A reduction from KRW 36 million to KRW 30 million places the proposed annual salary KRW 1.12 million below the general E-7-1 threshold for 2026.

If no separate occupation-specific rule applies, that is a concrete issue for the employer to address. HR should not proceed on the assumption that the employee's existing authorization makes the new salary irrelevant.

However, the fact that the revised salary is below the published threshold does **not** mean an existing E-7 status is automatically revoked on the day of the reduction.

The announcement establishes wage requirements used in immigration processes, including the issuance of confirmations of visa issuance. The consequences of a salary change for someone already authorized to work, and for a future extension of stay, must be evaluated under the applicable status-management rules and the employee's circumstances.

The employer should therefore establish which wage standard applies to the actual occupation, what remuneration is genuinely payable under the revised terms, and whether other particulars—such as the contract period—are also being changed.

### What if the salary increases?

Suppose the same employee's salary rises from KRW 36 million to KRW 40 million, while actual duties and worksite stay the same and the applicable wage criteria continue to be met.

A pay increase alone does not mean the employee must obtain a new E-7 status. But the employer still needs to distinguish the salary figure from any accompanying change in the payment structure, contract period or other statutorily reportable particulars.

Source: Ministry of Justice, Announcement No. 2025-406, 2026 Wage Requirements for Specific Activities (E-7) Status (December 29, 2025).

## 3. Immigration wage requirements are not the same as the employment contract's legal validity

Meeting an immigration wage threshold does not give an employer an unrestricted right to cut salary.

Article 17 of the Labor Standards Act (근로기준법) addresses the specification of statutory employment conditions, including wages, when an employment contract is concluded and when those conditions change. The law also contains specified exceptions to certain written-notice and delivery requirements.

For example, even if an employee's nominal annual compensation remains the same, replacing part of the fixed basic wage with performance-dependent pay may change the amount actually guaranteed. Where the employer intends to reduce wages, the employee's consent and the legal effect of that change must be considered separately.

**An immigration wage-eligibility check is different from a labor-law assessment of whether the contractual amendment is valid.** Preparation and submission of immigration-related administrative documents must also be distinguished from the legal analysis of labor disputes or an allegedly unfair transfer, which falls outside the Administrative Attorney's role in this context.

Source: National Law Information Center, Labor Standards Act, Article 17 (version effective October 8, 2026).

## 4. Must the employer report every salary change within 15 days?

**No. Changing the amount of salary is not independently listed as an employer-reporting event under Article 24(3) of the Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Control Act.**

Article 19 of the Immigration Control Act (출입국관리법) imposes reporting duties on employers in specified circumstances. Examples include dismissal, resignation, loss of contact with a foreign employee, and changes to important particulars of an employment contract.

The Enforcement Decree of the Immigration Control Act (출입국관리법 시행령), Article 24, specifies the relevant reporting triggers and the applicable 15-day period. Article 24(3) enumerates what counts as a change to important employment-contract particulars.

Those listed matters include changes to the employment contract period, certain changes to an employer's or worksite's name and address, and specified arrangements involving worker dispatch. The legislation also provides exclusions for particular circumstances.

The result is a meaningful distinction:

- **Salary only:** If the wage amount changes but no other legally listed event occurs, the salary change itself is not an independent trigger under Article 24(3).
- **Salary and contract period:** If the employer revises the wage and also changes the employment contract period, the period change must be assessed as its own potential reporting event.
- **Salary and worksite details:** If the amendment also involves an employer address or workplace change, the relevant employer-reporting and workplace-change provisions must be examined separately.

An agreement entitled "Amendment to Employment Contract" does not settle the reporting question. What matters is which legally specified facts actually changed.

For example, an agreement that increases annual pay from KRW 36 million to KRW 40 million while keeping the contract period unchanged is not legally identical to an agreement that amends both pay and contract duration.

The key question is not "Did we rewrite the contract?" It is **"Did any of the changes trigger a reporting obligation under the applicable law, and does an exclusion apply?"**

Source: National Law Information Center, Immigration Control Act, Article 19; Enforcement Decree, Article 24.

## 5. For a department transfer, examine actual duties rather than the job title

A company may have internal authority to reorganize departments and reassign staff. That does not establish that every task assigned to a foreign employee can be performed under the employee's current E-7 status.

Article 23 of the Immigration Control Act Enforcement Decree connects permitted employment activities to the activities covered by the person's status of stay. E-7 is a status for specific activities designated by the Minister of Justice.

### A name change without a substantive change in duties

Imagine that the original overseas-sales team is consolidated into an international-business team. The employee continues to manage overseas clients and export contracts.

In that situation, changing the department label does not, on its own, prove that the authorized scope of work has changed. HR should document the continuity between the duties previously approved and those now actually performed.

### A substantive change in duties

The analysis changes if the employee previously managed foreign accounts but is reassigned mainly to domestic sales or ordinary retail-store management.

Keeping "overseas sales" in a contract or job description cannot make a different set of actual duties consistent with the original approval. The relevant comparison is between the permitted occupation and the employee's real principal activities.

Where the role includes several kinds of work, distinguish its primary responsibilities from ancillary tasks. Describe the new duties accurately rather than rewriting the job description to conceal work that may fall outside the approval.

If the proposed activities fall outside the existing authorized scope, a separate immigration procedure may need to be considered. Depending on the circumstances, this can involve permission for activities outside the current status under Article 20 of the Immigration Control Act or permission to change status under Article 24.

This does **not** mean every change of duties within an E-7 employment arrangement automatically requires one of those permissions. The first step remains determining whether the employee's actual work would exceed the approved scope.

Source: National Law Information Center, Immigration Control Act Enforcement Decree, Article 23 and Appendix 1-2; Immigration Control Act, Articles 20 and 24.

## 6. The same legal employer does not make every worksite change equivalent

An HR transfer may take several legally distinct forms. The fact that the company name on the contract remains the same does not resolve which immigration procedure applies.

### Scenario A: The whole company relocates

Suppose a business moves its Seoul workplace to Gyeonggi Province, and the employee continues to work for that business in the same role.

The employer should determine whether the address change is a reportable worksite-relocation event under Article 24(3) of the Enforcement Decree, taking any exclusions into account. If an employer report is required, the company must confirm the relevant 15-day period and its starting date.

Moving the business as a whole is not the same as sending one employee to another establishment while the original head office remains in operation.

### Scenario B: Only one employee moves to a branch

Now suppose the company continues operating its Seoul headquarters but transfers one E-7 employee to its Gyeonggi branch.

The legal entity may be unchanged, yet the employee's actual workplace is different. HR should first identify the workplace authorized under the existing E-7 approval and determine whether the move constitutes a change or addition of workplace for immigration purposes.

Neither statement is safe as a blanket rule:

- "It is the same employer, so no immigration procedure can be necessary."
- "It is a branch transfer, so prior permission is always required."

The correct answer depends on the actual location, the approved particulars, the E-7 occupation and the applicable workplace-change rules.

### Scenario C: The employee works continuously at a client's premises

Consider an E-7 employee who remains on the original company's payroll but is assigned to work on an ongoing basis at a client business.

The salary payer remains the same, but the actual workplace and the person directing the employee's work may be different.

HR should distinguish a short business trip from a continuous placement at another business. It should establish where the employee works, what the employee does, and whose day-to-day instructions govern that work.

Whether an arrangement legally qualifies as worker dispatch and whether it is permitted under labor legislation require a separate assessment. Immigration authorization to work at that location must also be considered independently.

Listing the original company's address on the contract does not, by itself, authorize continuous work elsewhere.

## 7. The employer's report and the employee's workplace procedure are separate

Two different mechanisms often become confused during an internal transfer.

**First: employer reporting under Article 19.** This concerns whether an event specified by immigration law has occurred and whether the employer must report it within the statutory period.

**Second: workplace change or addition under Article 21.** This concerns the foreign employee's permission or reporting procedure when changing or adding a workplace.

For workplace changes governed by Article 21, prior permission is the general rule. There is a limited reporting exception under Article 26-2 of the Enforcement Decree for qualifying holders of E-1 through E-7 status who meet the conditions prescribed in the relevant Ministry of Justice notice.

Only employees who satisfy those conditions may use the exception and report the change within 15 days afterward. **An E-7 label by itself does not make a person eligible for post-change reporting.**

There is another important distinction. Article 24(3)(2)(d) of the Enforcement Decree excludes a workplace change made by the foreign employee under Article 21(1) from the particular employer-reporting event described there. The employer's reporting duty and the employee's workplace procedure therefore do not necessarily apply in duplicate.

A practical decision sequence is:

1. Decide whether the planned move is a change or addition of workplace under immigration law.
2. Determine whether the individual employee requires prior permission or meets the conditions for the 15-day post-change reporting exception.
3. Check separately whether the employer has a reportable event under Article 19.
4. Apply any statutory exclusion relevant to the two procedures.
5. If prior permission is required, obtain it before the employee begins working at the new workplace.

Source: National Law Information Center, Immigration Control Act, Articles 19 and 21; Enforcement Decree, Articles 24 and 26-2.

## 8. A five-step review when salary, duties and worksite change together

Return to the hypothetical employee whose employer remains unchanged:

- Current E-7 category: E-7-1.
- Annual salary: KRW 36 million, proposed to become KRW 30 million.
- Principal duties: overseas-sales work, proposed to become domestic-sales work.
- Workplace: Seoul headquarters, proposed to become a Gyeonggi branch.

Before implementing the reassignment, the employer can examine the proposal in five steps.

### Step 1: Establish the existing authorization

Confirm the E-7 subcategory, the occupation and principal activities previously approved, and the workplace covered by that approval.

Compare those particulars with the original employment contract and the work the employee has actually been doing. A document review is useful only if it reflects the employee's real position.

### Step 2: Compare the new salary with the applicable criteria

The proposed KRW 30 million salary is below the general 2026 E-7-1 annual threshold of KRW 31.12 million.

Determine whether a separate occupation-specific standard applies and what the change could mean under the employee's existing authorization and future extension procedures.

Review the employment-law consequences of reducing wages separately. Do not assume that the salary change itself triggers the employer's immigration report; check whether the contract period or another legally listed particular has also changed.

### Step 3: Check the proposed duties against the approved occupation

Compare the employee's former responsibility for overseas clients with the actual tasks expected in domestic sales.

HR should describe the principal and ancillary duties in the new job description. A transfer approved through the company's internal management process does not eliminate the need to assess the scope of activities permitted under E-7.

### Step 4: Classify the branch transfer and each applicable procedure

Identify the previously authorized workplace and the location of the proposed branch. Determine whether the move constitutes an immigration workplace change.

If it does, distinguish a prior-permission case from one qualifying for post-change reporting. Separately assess any employer report and any exclusion that avoids duplicated obligations.

### Step 5: Set an effective date consistent with the required procedure

Where prior workplace permission is required, the employee must not start working at the new workplace before obtaining it.

For a reporting case, verify the conditions and the deadline's starting point. Resolving the wage issue does not cure a change to activities outside the approved occupation. Equally, duties within scope do not remove a separate workplace obligation.

One compliant element does not automatically make the entire reassignment compliant.

## 9. If the reassignment has already happened, reconstruct the actual timeline

Suppose the employee has started working at the Gyeonggi branch and only then does HR discover that the company never assessed the immigration requirements.

The first task is to reconstruct what actually happened, not to create a new contract suggesting the move occurred on a different date.

Record the actual first day at the new workplace, the work carried out there, the date any new salary took effect, and the real payment terms. Then compare that timeline with the permission or reporting rules in force at the time.

An unreported change is not automatically a missed obligation if the law did not require a report in that situation. Conversely, if a required permission or report was omitted, issuing a new contract does not retrospectively discharge the original duty.

The next step depends on the verified facts and the procedure applicable to them. The employer should not disguise the actual history or assume that changing paperwork eliminates an earlier compliance problem.

## 10. What the employer can collect directly—and when an individual review is needed

A useful starting point is to assemble three sets of materials.

### Existing authorization and employment records

- The employee's E-7 subcategory and authorized period of stay.
- Records identifying the permitted occupation, approved duties and workplace.
- The current employment contract and material showing the work actually performed.

### Proposed terms and reassignment documents

- The proposed annual salary, its components and payment conditions.
- A job description explaining the main duties after the transfer.
- The proposed workplace, reassignment notice and intended effective date.

### Evidence of changes already implemented

- Payroll records and the date the new payment terms began.
- Actual responsibilities and the location where they were performed.
- The effective date of the transfer and other records of the implementation timeline.

The employer can gather these materials, compare the old and new terms, and review the published general wage thresholds itself.

An individual assessment becomes important where the occupation-specific wage rule is uncertain, where the new duties may exceed the existing authorization, or where a branch move raises a genuine question about permission, reporting or statutory exclusions. A past change that may have been implemented without a required procedure should likewise be examined against the actual dates.

There is also a professional boundary. Immigration-related administrative document preparation and submission must be distinguished from legal determinations about the contractual validity of a wage reduction, unfair reassignment or another labor dispute. The scope of any further work depends on both the issue identified and the services that an Administrative Attorney is legally permitted to provide.

## 11. Compare the actual employment arrangement with the existing E-7 approval

A change in salary, duties or workplace does not always require a new visa. Remaining with the same employer, however, does not mean that every change is automatically permitted.

The starting point is to compare three separate matters: the remuneration and wage rules, the actual work and authorized E-7 activities, and the workplace and applicable immigration procedure.

HR should also distinguish a salary adjustment from a contract-period change, and the employer's duty to report from the foreign employee's workplace permission or reporting procedure.

The employer's existing authorization records, employment contract, proposed job description and transfer date ordinarily provide the facts needed to identify these separate questions. Confirming any required prior procedure before setting the reassignment date avoids treating a signed contract as a substitute for immigration authorization.
